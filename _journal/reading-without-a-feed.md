Sometime in late 2024 I deleted the last social feed off my phone. I'd like to say it felt clean and freeing, but I spent the next few weeks reaching for the phone, swiping to where the app used to be, finding empty space, and not knowing what to do with the hand or the attention.

Withdrawal was the easy part. The hard part was that nothing else had been trained to fit the same hole. The five-minute window between meetings, the queue at the coffee shop, and the two minutes waiting for the kettle had been algorithmic-feed time for a decade. Without the feed they were just silence. Silence is fine, but a brain that's been on dopamine for ten years doesn't yet know what to do with it.

[WanderWiki](/apps/wanderwiki/) is the app in this catalogue I built most directly for that hand and that hole.

## What did I want from a reading app?

The brief I wrote for myself was small. I wanted an app that:

- Loaded fast enough to fill a thirty-second window without a loading spinner.
- Showed me something interesting on first launch without asking what I liked.
- Got *more* interesting if I told it what I liked, *without* tracking me.
- Worked on a flight without an "offline mode" hidden behind three menus.
- Didn't have a feed, or at least didn't try to be infinite.

Wikipedia was the obvious source. It is one of the largest collections of well-written, cited, consistently structured prose ever assembled, and it's available under a Creative Commons license. If I couldn't build a reading habit on Wikipedia, the fault would be mine.

## "For You" without tracking

Reading apps without a feed run into one problem: pure randomness gets boring quickly. Wikipedia has around seven million English articles, and [every language edition is a different encyclopedia](/journal/wikipedia-is-different-in-every-language/). A truly random tap will give you *Italian municipality of 800 people*, *American politician (1820-1888)*, and *insect taxonomy* in a row, and the user will close the app.

Most apps solve this by tracking what you read, building a profile, and using it to weight the recommendations. That works, and it's exactly what I deleted off my phone.

So can you have *For You* relevance without tracking? WanderWiki's answer is yes, if you ask the user what they like instead of building a profile behind their back. In a one-screen onboarding the user taps interest topics (*Science, History, Space, Architecture, Music, Food,* sixteen in all), and the For You feed draws its articles from the Wikipedia categories behind those declared preferences.

The user can change the topics any time. Press and hold a card and you can ask for less like it, mark a topic as not interesting, or hide the article, and For You takes that into account. All of it is worked out on the phone, and none of it leaves the phone. That makes it *less personalised* than a feed-based reader, but easier to correct: if the recommendations go off, you fix them with the topics and choices you made yourself.

## Today in History

The one piece of editorial scaffolding I did keep is the **Today in History** view. Switch to it and WanderWiki deals out what Wikipedia lists for the current calendar date: events, births, deaths, and holidays. A treaty in 1648. A first ascent in 1953. A scientific paper in 1979. A peaceful transition of power in 1994.

It works for the same reason it always has in newspapers: content tied to a date gives the brain a small handhold. It's *this day*, in another year, the same calendar slot occupied by a different version of the world. That handhold is why it stayed.

## Reading offline

The other constraint I held to was that the app should be some use on a plane.

Here WanderWiki only partly delivers. A discovery deck is live by nature: Random, For You, and Today in History all ask Wikipedia for something new, and on a plane there is no Wikipedia to ask. What WanderWiki does is keep what you save. Save an article on the ground and its full text downloads, so it is on the phone at cruising altitude, in the same list you use online, with no separate offline mode to switch on.

If you want the whole encyclopedia offline, that is a different engineering problem, and [Kiwix](/alternatives/kiwix/) solves it. WanderWiki handles the small reading list you build while wandering, which is still there on a 13-hour flight, when a feed replacement is most welcome.

## What it gives up

A reading app without an engagement feed or tracking is not going to outcompete Twitter for attention, and it shouldn't try. WanderWiki is made for short sessions, two articles or sometimes three, that end when you've had enough. Session length is the one number where I want WanderWiki to come in below the competition.

Daily active users would be a noisy number for an app like this anyway, and WanderWiki doesn't collect it. The better test is *did I just read something the algorithm would not have shown me, and did I close the app before I lost ten minutes I didn't mean to lose?*

## Where this fits in the catalogue

[WanderWiki](/apps/wanderwiki/) sits alongside a handful of other apps in this privacy-first catalogue that share one idea: the *replacement for the feed* doesn't have to be productive, but it should be quiet, finite, and yours.

It's a Saturday in early February, and the internet is doing whatever the internet does. If you've been looking for something to fill the small windows that used to be filled by something worse, you could do worse than three Wikipedia articles and a quiet kitchen.

*Update, 29 October 2026:* WanderWiki has grown since this was written. It now counts the days in a row you read something, on the Profile tab and in a widget. If you allow notifications, a daily one names the day's featured article, and an optional reminder warns you when a streak is about to break; you can retime or turn off both in Settings. The free plan stops at 20 articles a day, and Premium adds unlimited swiping, For You, and Today in History. For You still starts from the topics you pick, and the choices that adjust it stay on the phone.
