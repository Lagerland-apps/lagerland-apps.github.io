A list of bills answers *what do I owe this month*. The question that produces a short week is a different one: *which paycheck is paying for which bill*. A month can balance on paper, with income comfortably above bills, and still put $1,690 of bills on one paycheck and $330 on the next, because the rent, the car loan and the insurance all land in the same fortnight.

A bill calendar answers the second question. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes a printable [bill calendar tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_bill_calendar_tool_2018-11_ADA.pdf) in its *Your Money, Your Goals* toolkit, and the method works even if you never print it.

## The bill calendar method, step by step

| Step | What to do | The rule that makes it work |
|---|---|---|
| 1. List every bill | Rent, utilities, loans, insurance, phone, subscriptions | Include autopays; they still need cash in the account |
| 2. Write the pay-by date | The day the money has to leave your account, not the due date | CFPB: 2 to 3 days early for online or in-person payments, at least 7 days early by mail |
| 3. Add your paydays | Every date money arrives, with the amount | Use that month's real dates, not "twice a month" |
| 4. Total each pay period | Add the bills whose pay-by date falls between one payday and the next | Compare each total with the paycheck that has to cover it |
| 5. Fix the heavy period | Ask to move a due date, or use a bills account | The CFPB tool suggests asking to move due dates when a week's bills outrun its income |
| 6. Recheck monthly | Look at next month before it starts | Biweekly paydays move, so April's calendar can fail in May |

Step 2 does most of the work. The CFPB's tool is plain about the cost of treating the due date as the target: pay even a few days late and you will likely be charged a fee, and you risk a negative entry in your credit record.

## Why biweekly pay breaks a monthly calendar

Biweekly is the most common pay schedule in the US. In February 2023, 43.0% of private establishments paid every two weeks, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ces/publications/length-pay-period.htm). It is also the schedule that fits a monthly calendar worst, because 14 days does not divide into a month. Your paydays land two or three days earlier in the month each month, and twice a year a month gets three of them.

A worked example. Paid every other Friday, with a payday on April 3, 2026, and nine monthly bills totalling $2,080: rent $1,200 on the 1st, car loan $310 on the 6th, phone $55 on the 9th, car insurance $125 on the 12th, internet $60 on the 15th, electricity $90 on the 20th, student loan $160 on the 25th, two streaming services $36 on the 27th, gym $44 on the 28th.

With each pay-by date set three days before the due date, this is what each paycheck has to cover:

| Payday | Bills whose pay-by date falls before the next payday | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Fri Apr 3 | Car loan, phone, car insurance, internet | $550 |
| Fri Apr 17 | Electricity, student loan, streaming, gym, May rent | $1,530 |
| Fri May 1 | Car loan, phone, car insurance, internet | $550 |
| Fri May 15 | Electricity, student loan, streaming, gym | $330 |
| Fri May 29 | June rent, car loan, phone, car insurance | $1,690 |
| Fri Jun 12 | Internet, electricity, student loan, streaming, gym | $390 |
| Fri Jun 26 | July rent, car loan, phone, car insurance | $1,690 |

Same bills, same pay, and the load on one paycheck swings from $330 to $1,690. Across 2026 on this schedule it runs from $275 (August 7) to $1,750 (August 21).

Three things fall out of the table:

- **Rent is never paid by its own month's paycheck.** Rent due on the 1st has a pay-by date in the last days of the previous month, so it always comes out of a paycheck from the month before.
- **The third paycheck is not spare money.** May 2026 has three paydays on this schedule. The third, May 29, lands just before June's rent and ties for the heaviest load in the table.
- **Which months get three paydays depends on your Fridays.** Twenty-six biweekly paydays is 24 plus two extras. On this cadence they fall in May and October; on the alternate Fridays, starting January 2, in January and July. Weekly pay on Fridays gives 52 paydays in 2026, with five in January, May, July and October.

## Two ways to fix a lopsided calendar

**Move a due date.** With semimonthly pay (the 1st and 15th, say) this is the whole fix: paydays never move, so each paycheck covers the same bills every month, and if a lender agrees to move one due date, the balance holds for good. With biweekly pay it helps less, because the paycheck that is overloaded this month is a different one next month.

**Smooth it with a fixed transfer.** Keep a separate account just for bills, and on every payday move the same amount into it: monthly bills × 12 ÷ 26. Here that is $2,080 × 12 ÷ 26 = **$960 per paycheck**. Twenty-six transfers of $960 make $24,960, exactly twelve months of $2,080. Pay every bill from that account and the swing disappears from your spending money: each paycheck gives up $960, whatever the calendar does.

The account needs a starting cushion, because the money arrives evenly and the bills do not. For this schedule, seeding it with one $960 transfer in January, after January's rent, keeps it positive all year. Its lowest point is $320 on April 28, the pay-by date for May's rent, and it ends the year back at $960.

## Credit cards: the one due date that stays put

Card due dates are the most predictable line on the calendar. Under Regulation Z, a card's due date must fall on the same day of the month in every billing cycle ([12 CFR 1026.7(b)(11)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/7/)). The statement must be mailed or delivered at least 21 days before that date ([§ 1026.5(b)(2)(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/5/)). And the issuer cannot set a payment cut-off earlier than 5 p.m. on the due date ([§ 1026.10(b)(2)(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/10/)). Enter the card once and it will not wander.

## If a date slips anyway

The threshold worth knowing is 30 days. The CFPB's guide to [reading your credit report](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_understand_your_credit_report_handout.pdf) says an account is usually still reported as current if it is paid within 30 days of the due date. A late fee can arrive much sooner, depending on your agreement. Past that point, negative payment history can generally be reported for [up to seven years](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-information-stay-on-my-credit-report-en-323/). A payment you notice on day three is a fee problem. On day thirty-one it can become a credit-history problem.

Ask Siri "What bills are due in AllPaid" and it reads out the next seven days.

Two decisions are deliberate. A bill whose date has gone by is labelled *Due date passed*, not *overdue*, because the app's design brief asks for calm, neutral language and rules out overdue panic states. That second decision is also its limit here. AllPaid does not know when you are paid and has no income field, so steps 3 to 5 (paydays, pay-period totals, the transfer amount) stay on paper or in a note. It also cannot remind you about a bill you never entered.

## TL;DR

- **A bill calendar shows which paycheck pays which bill.** A balanced month can still hide one overloaded fortnight.
- **Plan around pay-by dates, not due dates.** The CFPB suggests 2 to 3 days early online and at least 7 days early by mail.
- **Biweekly pay drifts.** In the example, the same $2,080 of bills puts anywhere from $330 to $1,690 on a single paycheck.
- **The smoothing formula is monthly bills × 12 ÷ 26.** Move that much to a bills account every payday, and seed it with one transfer.
- **Card due dates are fixed by rule:** same day every month, statement 21 days ahead, cut-off no earlier than 5 p.m.
- **Paid within 30 days, an account is usually still reported current.** Fees can start sooner.
