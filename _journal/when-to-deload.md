A deload is a short, planned stretch of easier training, usually about a week, taken to shed fatigue before you push again. The common version keeps your exercises and training days and changes three things:

1. Fewer sets, often roughly half.
2. Lighter loads, especially on the big multi-joint lifts.
3. More reps in reserve: you stop each set further from failure.

When to take one is the harder question. Here is what the research and the people who coach for a living say.

## What is a deload, by definition?

Until recently there wasn't an agreed definition. In 2023, Bell and colleagues ran a [Delphi consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/) with expert strength and physique coaches over three rounds (34 coaches started, 21 finished). The definition they arrived at describes a deload as a period of reduced training stress designed to reduce fatigue, promote recovery and prepare the athlete for the training that follows. They separated it from a taper, which is about peaking for a competition.

The panel agreed on surprisingly little beyond that. Reducing volume was the only point with universal agreement. Intensity could go down, stay the same, or even rise, depending on the athlete and the sport. Deloads could be planned in advance, triggered by how the athlete feels, or both.

## What are the signs you need a deload?

The best data on real-world triggers comes from [Rogerson and colleagues' 2024 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/) of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, most of them powerlifters.

| Trigger | Athletes who used it |
|---|---|
| The program specifies a deload | 65.4% |
| Feeling beaten up (soreness, joint aches, pain) | 62.6% |
| Performance stalls or drops | 54.1% |
| Previous injuries flare up | 31.3% |
| High stress outside training | 30.5% |
| Holiday or travel | 24.4% |
| Not feeling like training | 14.6% |

Turned into something you can check on a Sunday evening, the signs worth watching are:

- Several lifts have stopped moving at once. One stalled lift usually needs a programming change. Three stalled lifts at the same time point at fatigue.
- Your usual weights rate harder. If a load that was an RPE 7 a month ago now feels like an 8.5, on several lifts, for more than one session, that is a recovery signal. [How RPE and reps in reserve work](/journal/rpe-and-rir-explained/) covers how to rate that honestly.
- Joints and tendons ache before the session starts, not just after it.
- Life outside the gym is taking more than usual: a heavy work stretch, a new baby, bad sleep.

One of these on its own is a bad week. Two or more together for a week or longer is what most deload rules are written for.

A note on pain: a deload is for fatigue. Pain that is sharp, localised or getting worse is a different problem, and the right person to see about it is a clinician, not your program.

## How do you run a deload?

There are four common shapes. Most real deloads combine the first three.

| Scheme | What changes | What stays the same | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume deload | Fewer sets, often about half; sometimes fewer reps per set | Exercises, training days, loads close to normal | High-volume and hypertrophy-focused training |
| Intensity deload | Lighter loads | Sets, reps, exercises | Heavy, low-rep strength phases |
| Effort deload | Stop further from failure, more reps in reserve | Program structure | Programs written in RPE or RIR |
| Week off | No lifting at all | Nothing, apart from walking or other sport | Travel, illness, or when you need a mental break |

The survey shows what athletes actually do. During a deload, 78.9% cut their weekly sets and 52.8% cut reps per set. On multi-joint lifts, 83.7% used lighter loads and 84.9% stopped further from failure. Meanwhile 63.0% kept the same number of sessions per week, and 70.3% kept the same multi-joint exercises. The pattern is clear enough to copy: same days, same main lifts, less of everything.

Some programs build the deload into the structure. 5/3/1's fourth week is one, as the table in [the program-choosing post](/journal/choosing-a-strength-program-you-will-finish/) shows.

## How often should you deload?

The survey's athletes deloaded every 5.6 weeks on average, with a spread of plus or minus 2.3 weeks and a range from 1 to 12, and each deload lasted about 6.4 days. The Delphi paper describes pre-planned deloads every 4 to 8 weeks as common practice, while its panel declined to set a fixed interval and said frequency should follow how the athlete responds.

Planning one every 4 to 6 weeks and moving it when the signs above show up is a reasonable default. Bring it forward when several signs line up early; push it back a week when training is going well and nothing aches. A deload taken because the calendar said so, in a week when you feel great, costs you little, but it isn't buying much either.

## What does the evidence actually show?

Less than the confidence of most gym advice would suggest. Most of what we know is expert opinion (the Delphi panel) and self-report (the survey), and the survey's own authors call their findings broad guidelines until experiments test whether deloading helps. In that survey, 65.0% of athletes said they felt they could progress without deloading at all.

The controlled trials so far are small:

- [Coleman and colleagues (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/) had 39 resistance-trained adults run a 9-week, high-volume program. One group took the whole of week 5 off. Muscle growth, power and local endurance came out about the same in both groups. The group that trained straight through gained somewhat more lower-body strength (squat 1RM rose by about 16 kg against 13 kg), though the statistical uncertainty around that difference included zero. Note that this was a full week off, not a reduced-load deload.
- [Pancar and colleagues (2026)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13031491/) trained 19 untrained young men, with each person's arms and legs randomly assigned to continuous training or to a deload of one session and two sets per exercise in weeks 4 and 8. Muscle thickness and 10-rep strength improved similarly under both conditions.

Put together: a well-run deload doesn't appear to cost muscle, a complete week off may cost a little top-end strength, and no trial I know of has shown that deloads make you stronger over the long run. They remain a sensible way to manage fatigue and to make hard training sustainable. Treat any deload schedule as a starting point you adjust.

## How GymLogger X schedules deloads and flags plateaus

[GymLogger X](/apps/gymlogger-x/) handles deloads in three places.

In the programs it builds, the Smart Program Creator schedules a deload week every 4 weeks for beginners, every 5 for intermediates and every 6 for advanced lifters in linear, undulating and wave programs. Block programs end each block with one. A deload week halves the sets (never below one per exercise), keeps the rep ranges, and raises the reps-in-reserve target, by three in most program styles, so the loads come down with the effort. That matches what most of the surveyed athletes did.

In your history, sessions logged during a program's deload week are marked as deloads automatically, and any other session can be marked as one from its menu. The flag matters because a light week would otherwise drag down your strength trend and your weekly set counts. The app also notices unplanned deloads: if your weekly training volume falls by 30% or more after two hard weeks, the recovery screen marks that week on its load chart and tells you how long ago your last deload was.

In the plateau rule, a Pro feature, deload sessions are dropped before the rule runs, so a deliberately light week can't create a false stall. Detection stays off for your first 12 weeks of logged training and needs at least eight sessions on a lift, or five if you train it rarely. It then fits a trend through the estimated 1RM of your four most recent sessions on that lift, using the Epley formula from [the one-rep max post](/journal/how-to-estimate-your-one-rep-max/), and compares it with a threshold built from your own rate of gain over the last 90 days, kept between 0.05% and 1% per session. If the trend misses that bar while volume hasn't dropped by more than 3%, three sessions earn a Monitoring flag. A confirmed plateau needs four, plus either a fatigue sign (training the lift twice in a week, on back-to-back days, or with volume rising while strength stays flat) or at least six non-deload sessions logged on that lift, and a confidence score of at least 0.4.

A confirmed flag lists a likely cause and two suggestions. When volume has been climbing, it suggests cutting volume by about 20% or taking a deload week; when the cause is unclear, it suggests a deload week or a change of rep range. It doesn't move your program for you. Whether this is the week to deload is still your call, and the signs above are the ones I'd check first.
