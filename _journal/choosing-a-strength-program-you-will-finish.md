Most program arguments are about the wrong variable.

Ask whether PPL beats Upper/Lower and you get a hundred confident answers about frequency, volume distribution and recovery. All of it is real. Almost none of it is decisive. Run two competently written programs, same person, same twelve weeks, and you would struggle to see the difference in your own logbook.

What decides the outcome is whether you are still running the thing in week ten: the Tuesday you leave the office at 19:40, the fortnight in March when everyone in the house is ill. So the comparison below is built around days per week, session length, and the specific way each program breaks.

## Which strength program should you run?

| Program | Days/week | Realistic session | Progression model | Frequency per muscle | Suits | Characteristic failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full body 3×** | 3 | 50–70 min | Linear, then double progression | ~3× | Beginners, unpredictable weeks, returning lifters | Sessions creep longer as the squat gets heavy. Near-maximal squatting three times a week stops being recoverable, and people quit rather than reduce. |
| **Upper/Lower 4×** | 4 | 55–75 min | Double progression, or percentage blocks | 2× | Intermediates with four genuinely reliable slots | The fourth day gets dropped. The split silently becomes a lopsided three-day where legs are trained once. |
| **PPL 6×** | 6 | 45–75 min | Double progression, usually per exercise | 2× | People who want six gym days and have a short commute | Miss two sessions and a fixed weekly rotation desynchronises. The make-it-up logic collapses inside a fortnight. |
| **Starting Strength / novice LP** | 3 | 45–75 min, growing | Linear: add load every session | 3× | Untrained or detrained lifters, roughly the first 3–9 months of barbell work | The linear phase ends and nobody notices. Lifters reset the same 10% three or four times instead of changing model. |
| **5/3/1** | 3–4 | 40–60 min plus assistance | Percentage-based off a Training Max, 4-week cycle | 1× per main lift, ~2× per muscle with assistance | Intermediates wanting a long runway and few weekly decisions | It feels light in month one, so the Training Max gets inflated. The AMRAP set becomes a grinder and stops adding reps. |

**Weekly frequency per muscle is not what separates these programs.** Full body and PPL sit at three and two, and once weekly volume is held equal the frequency research is not dramatic in either direction. Greg Nuckols' [review of the frequency literature at Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-frequency/) is the most careful public summary I know of. Twice per muscle per week is a fine default, not a law.

**The failure modes, by contrast, are specific.** PPL does not fail because six days is too much volume; it fails because six fixed slots is too many places for a week to break. Novice linear progression fails because it is designed to end, and almost nobody has a plan for after.

## What does each progression model actually tell you to do?

The split is the easy half. The progression model decides what you do on a Tuesday when the bar feels heavy, and it is where most self-written programs go vague.

| Model | What you add | When you add it | On a failed session | Deload rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Linear** | Load | Every session: typically 2.5 kg / 5 lb upper body, 5 kg / 10 lb lower body early on | Repeat the same weight next session. After two or three consecutive failures, reset ~10% and build back | The reset *is* the deload. The classic novice template has no scheduled deload weeks |
| **Double progression** | Reps first, then load | Add load only when every set hits the top of the bracket. In 3 × 8–12: stay at the weight until all three sets reach 12, then add the smallest increment and drop back to 8 | Nothing special. Repeat the load and try for one more rep. A failed increase is a normal week | Cut load ~10% or drop a set for a week when two or three lifts stall simultaneously |
| **Percentage / Training Max (5/3/1)** | A fixed increment to the *Training Max*, not to your 1RM | Once per completed cycle: +2.5 kg / 5 lb upper, +5 kg / 10 lb lower | If the AMRAP set falls below the prescribed minimum reps, hold the Training Max or cut it ~10% next cycle | Built in. Week four is the deload at 40/50/60% of the Training Max |
| **Autoregulated / RPE** | Load, chosen to hit a target RPE or reps-in-reserve | Every set. A top set at RPE 8, back-offs a set percentage below it | There is no failed session, only a lighter one. The load that hits RPE 8 today is the prescription | Deload when the load at a given RPE drops for two or more sessions, or on a fixed 4–6 week cadence |

A worked example, because double progression is the one people describe correctly and then run incorrectly. Dumbbell rows, 3 sets of 8 to 12, at 30 kg. Session one: 12, 10, 9 — no increase, two sets missed the top. Session two: 12, 12, 10. Still no. Session three: 12, 12, 12. Now you take the smallest increment your gym has, go to 32 kg, and expect roughly 9, 8, 8. That is not a step backwards. The bracket resets and you climb it again, and it may take five weeks.

## What is 5/3/1, exactly?

5/3/1 comes from [Jim Wendler](https://www.jimwendler.com/blogs/jimwendler-com/101065094-5-3-1-for-a-beginner), and its central idea is the Training Max: you programme off 85-90% of your tested one-rep max — the original template uses 90% — so the percentages stay honest on a bad week. Every percentage below is of the Training Max, not your actual max.

| Week | Set 1 | Set 2 | Top set |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 65% × 5 | 75% × 5 | 85% × 5+ |
| 2 | 70% × 3 | 80% × 3 | 90% × 3+ |
| 3 | 75% × 5 | 85% × 3 | 95% × 1+ |
| 4 (deload) | 40% × 5 | 50% × 5 | 60% × 5 |

The "+" is the whole program. That last set is taken for as many quality reps as you have, and its rep count is your progress signal — the fixed sets are the same percentages forever. Finish the cycle, add 2.5 kg to the upper-body Training Maxes and 5 kg to the lower-body ones, run it again. Roughly a year of programming decided in one sitting, which is the appeal.

The failure mode is self-inflicted. Month one feels easy, so people set the Training Max at their real max, or add 5 kg a cycle to everything. Six cycles later the top set is one grinding rep. Wendler's own advice is worth taking: start lighter than you think.

## How many days a week should you lift?

The floor is well established. The US [Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines) put muscle-strengthening activity at two or more days a week for adults, covering all major muscle groups. That is a health floor, not a strength target.

For building strength, three to five days is where most sustainable programs live. But pick your number backwards, not forwards. Open whatever record you have of the last eight weeks and count the sessions. If it is 19, you train two and a bit days a week, and a six-day PPL is not a program, it is a wish. Add a day only after the existing days have been boring and reliable for a month.

The most common mistake I see is people picking a program for the version of themselves that exists in January.

## When does a novice linear progression end?

Novice linear progression — [Mark Rippetoe's Starting Strength](https://startingstrength.com/) is the best-known implementation — works because an untrained lifter recovers between sessions in a couple of days and adapts fast enough to add load every session. That window is real, and it closes, usually somewhere between three and nine months of consistent barbell work depending on age, food and sleep.

The published stall rule is to repeat the weight, then after two or three consecutive failed sessions reset roughly 10% and build back. What the rule cannot tell you is how many times to do that.

The heuristic I use: **two resets on the same lift with no new personal record between them means the linear phase is over for that lift.** Not for you, and not for the other lifts. The press stalls long before the deadlift, so the press moves to double progression while the squat keeps running linear. A lifter can be a novice on one bar and an intermediate on another for months, and treating "am I a beginner?" as one global fact is what keeps people resetting the same 10% four times.

## How to choose, in four questions

1. **How many days did you actually train in the last eight weeks?** Count them, divide by eight. That is your days per week — not your intention, not your best month. Three means full body. Four means Upper/Lower. Six means PPL, if and only if six is a number you have hit before.
2. **How long is a session on your worst weekday?** Under 50 minutes and two things fit: full body three times a week, or 5/3/1 with trimmed assistance. A 75-minute PPL leg day does not shrink to 45 minutes; it becomes a skipped one.
3. **What is your training age in this specific lift?** Under six months of consistent barbell work: linear. Six months to two years: double progression. Beyond: percentage cycles or autoregulation, and expect progress measured per quarter.
4. **What has hurt, and what made it hurt?** If flat barbell benching aggravates a shoulder, no split solves that. Pick a structure that lets you swap the movement without deleting a day — organised by movement pattern, not by a fixed list of named exercises.

This is training information, not medical advice; if you are working around an injury or a condition that affects loading, get eyes on it from a clinician or a coach who can see you lift.

## Why the log is the part that decides it

Everything above assumes you know what you did last time. That assumption is doing more work than any of the programming.

Linear progression is the only model on that second table you can run from memory, because the rule is *last time plus 2.5 kg*. Every other one is a rule applied to a record. Double progression needs three set counts from last session. A 5/3/1 cycle needs a Training Max and every AMRAP rep count going back months. Autoregulation needs a history of what a given RPE cost you in kilos, or the number is a feeling with a decimal point attached. ## TL;DR

- **Adherence beats programming.** Between two competent programs run twelve weeks the difference is small; between finishing and quitting in week five it is enormous.
- **Days per week is a measurement, not a decision.** Count the sessions you did over the last eight weeks and divide by eight. Three: full body. Four: Upper/Lower. Six: PPL, only if you have hit six before.
- **Each split has one characteristic failure.** PPL desynchronises after two missed sessions. Upper/Lower loses its fourth day. Full body's sessions grow until heavy squatting three times a week stops being recoverable. Novice linear progression ends and nobody notices.
- **Match the progression model to training age, per lift.** Linear under six months, double progression to roughly two years, then 5/3/1-style percentage cycles or RPE autoregulation.
- **Double progression in one line:** stay at the weight until every set hits the top of the bracket, then add the smallest increment and drop back to the bottom.
- **Only linear progression runs from memory.** Every other model is a rule applied to a written record, which is why the log, not the template, is what finishes the twelve weeks.
