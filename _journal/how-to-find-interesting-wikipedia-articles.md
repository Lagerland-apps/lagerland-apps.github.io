The Random article link in Wikipedia's sidebar does exactly what it says, and that is its limit. English Wikipedia has more than seven million articles. About 7,000 of them are featured articles, the grade the community gives its best writing: roughly one in a thousand. Pressing Random is a good way to meet the encyclopedia and a slow way to find its best pages.

Wikipedia has better-aimed routes built in. Here they are, from fully random to hand-picked, followed by how to combine them.

## Every built-in way to find something to read

| Route | Where to find it | What you get | How it is chosen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random article | Sidebar link, or Special:Random | Any article | A random number matched against an index (below) |
| Random featured article | Special:RandomInCategory/Featured_articles | One of about 7,000 featured articles | Random within the category |
| Random good article | Special:RandomInCategory/Good_articles | One of more than 40,000 good articles | Random within the category |
| Today's featured article | Main Page | One featured article a day | Scheduled by editors |
| Did you know | Main Page | One-line hooks to articles that are new, newly expanded or newly promoted | Nominated and reviewed; usually one set a day |
| On this day | Main Page | Anniversaries for today's date | Picked from a queue; quality and topic mix count |
| Unusual articles | Wikipedia:Unusual articles | A long list of odd but verifiable articles, grouped by subject | Collected by editors |
| Vital articles | Wikipedia:Vital articles | The topics editors think most deserve a strong article, in levels of 10 to 50,000 | Agreed by editors |

Random article has a keyboard shortcut: Alt+Shift+X in Firefox, Alt+X in Chrome and Edge, as Wikipedia's [Random help page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Random) lists them. Special:Random also works in other language editions; on German Wikipedia it redirects to Spezial:Zufällige Seite.

## How random is the Random article link?

Random enough to browse, not random enough to sample. Wikipedia's [technical FAQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:FAQ/Technical) explains the mechanism. Every page is given a random index, a number between 0 and 1. Special:Random draws its own random number and returns the next article whose index is higher. An article that sits just after a wide gap between indexes gets picked more often than one close behind its neighbour, so every article's odds differ slightly, and the differences are themselves random. The FAQ's verdict: good enough for a small sample.

The pool is the main namespace: every article, long or short, reviewed or not. Run the counts from Wikipedia's own pages and a random click lands like this:

| Kind of article | How many (English Wikipedia) | Roughly one click in |
|---|---|---|
| Featured article | about 7,000 | 1,000 |
| Good article | more than 40,000 | 160 |
| Any article | more than 7 million | 1 |

Counts as [Wikipedia:Featured articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_articles) and [Wikipedia:Good articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Good_articles) gave them in April 2026. Both move every week, so check those pages for live figures.

More than 99 clicks in 100 land on an article that is neither featured nor good. Plenty of those are excellent and simply unreviewed, but if you want the reviewed ones, there is a better button.

## How to aim the randomness

Special:RandomInCategory takes a category name and returns a random page from that category ([MediaWiki help](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Help:RandomInCategory)). Wikipedia files every featured and good article in a maintenance category, which turns it into a quality filter:

- **A random featured article:** [Special:RandomInCategory/Featured_articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:RandomInCategory/Featured_articles). Before they are listed, featured articles are reviewed as featured article candidates for accuracy, neutrality, completeness and style. On the desktop site they carry a small bronze star at the top right.
- **A random good article:** [Special:RandomInCategory/Good_articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:RandomInCategory/Good_articles). Good articles meet a core set of editorial standards. They need not be as comprehensive as featured articles, but they should not omit any major facet of the subject. They carry a green plus icon.

Bookmark those two addresses and you have Random buttons that only land on reviewed articles. The address works with other categories too: put a different category name after the slash.

## The hand-picked routes

The Main Page is the other way in, and each of its sections runs on rules worth knowing.

**Today's featured article** is one featured article a day, so it clears the same bar as the random featured link.

**Did you know** is where new writing surfaces. An article qualifies if it was created, expanded fivefold or promoted to good article in the seven days before its nomination, and it needs at least 1,500 characters of original prose ([the rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Did_you_know)). Each entry is a one-sentence hook linking to the article, and the Main Page normally runs one new set a day.

**On this day** draws on [Selected anniversaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Selected_anniversaries), a queue of historical events, holidays and observances for each date. Importance is not the only test: editors also weigh article quality and the mix of topics.

**[Unusual articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Unusual_articles)** is a long list, kept by editors, of articles that are verifiable and worth having but odd or whimsical. Its sections run from places and infrastructure to food, folklore and death. It is the nearest thing Wikipedia has to a curated collection of the strange.

**[Vital articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Vital_articles)** works the other way round. It lists the subjects editors think most deserve a high-quality article, in five levels from the 10 most vital up to 50,000, which makes it a reading list for the central topics rather than the edges.

## A routine that uses all of it

1. **Five spare minutes:** press Random a few times and stop at the first article you want to finish.
2. **Wanting a sure thing:** use the featured or good article links above.
3. **Once a day:** read the Did you know hooks and On this day, which change daily.
4. **When something grabs you:** follow the categories at the bottom of the article. Each one is a shelf of neighbouring subjects, and any of them can go after the slash in RandomInCategory.

## Where WanderWiki fits

[WanderWiki](/apps/wanderwiki/) puts three of these routes into a card deck on iPhone. **Random** asks Wikipedia's REST API for a random article, the API's version of the Random article link, so anything in the encyclopedia can come up. **For You** draws from topics you choose, which narrows the draw the way a category does, without watching what you linger on. **Today in History** shows what happened on today's date. Swipe to the next card, or open one to read the full article reformatted for the phone, and save the ones you want to keep.

There is no account and no advertising. The [privacy page](/apps/wanderwiki/privacy/) sets out that saved articles and interests are stored on the device and that articles come from Wikipedia's public APIs. Why it is a deck you put down rather than a feed you scroll is the subject of [an earlier post](/journal/reading-without-a-feed/).

What the app does not try to replace is the website. If what you want is specifically a random featured article, the RandomInCategory address above is the most direct route there is.
