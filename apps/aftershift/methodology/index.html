Shift Recovery Score —
how it is actually calculated.

The Shift Recovery Score is the metric AfterShift is built around. It is not a sleep score in the consumer sense; it is a forward-looking estimate of how recovered you are before your next scheduled shift. This page explains the three inputs and their weights, the two caps, what the score deliberately does not do, and what the literature on shift-work fatigue actually backs.

What the score is — and is not.

What it is
  • A single 0–100 estimate for the window between your last shift and your next one, updated as new sleep comes in
  • Forward-looking: the question is "how recovered are you for the next block?", not "how good was last night?"
  • Shift-aware: measured between your actual shifts, not over a 9-to-5 day
  • Nap-aware: pre-shift and recovery naps actually move the number
  • Informational: meant to inform a decision, not to make one for you
What it deliberately is not
  • Not a medical assessment and not a diagnosis of Shift Work Disorder
  • Not a replacement for clinical sleep evaluation if your sleep is significantly impaired
  • Not a productivity score, fitness score, or generic wellness number
  • Not derived from heart rate or heart-rate variability — AfterShift reads only sleep data from Apple Health
  • Not benchmarked against other users; the score is measured against your own sleep target

The three inputs — and your next shift.

Every Shift Recovery Score is a weighted combination of three signals, measured over the window from the end of your last shift to the start of your next one. Your next shift then sets two caps. The weights are fixed in the app; we update this page when they change.

01
Sleep amount, naps included — 50%
The biggest input is simply how much you slept in the window, against the sleep target you set (treated as at least four hours), up to 100%. Every sleep counts — the main block and every nap — measured as time actually asleep, so a nap you logged by hand and one imported from Apple Health count the same way. A daytime sleep after a night shift counts exactly as much as the same hours at night; the score does not care what the clock said. Nap timing is analysed separately (Premium shows nap timing and grogginess patterns), but it does not change the score.
02
Sleep continuity — 30%
A six-hour sleep broken three times is structurally different from an unbroken six hours, and the literature on shift-work fatigue treats them as different. AfterShift looks at your longest unbroken stretch — four hours or more earns full credit, and shorter stretches earn progressively less — then subtracts a small penalty when you woke three or more times, growing at six and at ten awakenings. This needs a tracker that writes sleep detail to Apple Health; without it, continuity uses a neutral default. The Trends view shows whether your fragmentation is getting better or worse from cycle to cycle.
03
Stage balance — Deep and REM — 20%
From Apple Health, AfterShift reads the breakdown of your sleep into Awake, Core, Deep, and REM, and scores the share of the window's sleep spent in Deep and REM: 25% or more earns full credit, with lower bands below 25%, 18%, and 12%. Deep sleep at 11 a.m. after a night shift counts the same as Deep sleep at 3 a.m. If your tracker records how long you slept but not stages — or you only log sleep by hand — this input uses a neutral default instead of penalising you.
04
Your next shift — two caps
The window ends when your next shift starts, so a quick turnaround leaves less room to recover. If the whole gap between shifts is shorter than your sleep target, the score is capped at 79 — the top of "Good" — because a full night was never possible (days off are exempt). If the window includes an on-call shift, the score is capped at 75, because unpredictable hours limit how recovered anyone can be. Labels: Poor 0–39, Fair 40–59, Good 60–79, Excellent 80–100.

What the literature does — and does not — support.

Honest framing of what we are confident about, what we are using as a working model, and what is contested.

Well-established in the literature
  • Rotating and night shifts impair sleep continuity and total sleep time relative to day work — robustly replicated
  • Prophylactic naps before night shifts reduce subjective fatigue and improve performance metrics during the shift
  • The circadian low between roughly 02:00 and 06:00 is the period of highest fatigue and lowest alertness, almost independent of recent sleep
  • Caffeine half-life is roughly 5 hours in average adults — meaningful for cutoff timing
  • Forward rotation (day → evening → night) generally adapts better than backward rotation
Working models, not settled science
  • Exact weights for sleep amount vs continuity vs stage composition in a recovery score — there is no consensus formula in the literature; ours (50 / 30 / 20) is a design judgement, not a fitted model
  • Optimal nap length for a given shift type — broad guidance exists (short-restorative ~20 min vs full-cycle ~90 min); precise individual optima are not pinned down
  • How much a single bad night within a block actually costs compared to a chronically short week — modelled, not measured per individual

For the underlying science, we recommend the U.S. NIOSH shift work training module and the AASM ICSD-3 entry on Shift Work Disorder as starting points. Both are written for clinicians but readable for technically minded shift workers.

How the score should — and should not — change your behaviour.

Use it to inform a decision
  • Decide whether to take a pre-shift nap, and roughly how long
  • Decide when to stop caffeine before your post-shift sleep window
  • Spot the weakest day inside a rotation block before fatigue compounds
  • Talk to a clinician with data, not a vague "I feel tired"
Do not use it to
  • Diagnose a sleep disorder — see a sleep specialist if your sleep is significantly impaired
  • Decide whether to drive after a shift — that is a binary safety judgment, not a 0–100 score
  • Optimise yourself into never feeling tired — shift work imposes real biological costs that no app can eliminate
  • Compare yourself to other AfterShift users; the score is measured against your own sleep target
